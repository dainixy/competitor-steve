## 01 — Design Thinking: Overview

- **Origin:** the process was developed by David Kelley (transcript says "Kelly"), founder of IDEO — a firm that works across industrial, product, and service design. Design thinking only spread into mainstream business in the last few years, specifically *because* of the outcomes it produced elsewhere first — it's an imported process, not a native business tool.
- **Cupcake/frosting analogy** (use this to explain the value of process to a non-designer): your base craft output — the PSD, the sketch file, the shipped site — is the cupcake. Design thinking is the frosting layered on top. Without the frosting, the cupcake is dull. The frosting is what you're actually being paid for, not the batter.
- **Named risk of skipping process:** working from intuition alone means you are "designing in the dark." Intuition isn't rejected — it's coupled to a feedback loop with real users so you can course-correct, not left to run unchecked.
- **Jigsaw puzzle analogy for phases 1–2:** Discovery = collecting puzzle pieces (raw, disparate data). Interpretation = arranging those pieces into the actual picture (synthesized information). Useful as a one-line explanation to clients for why discovery precedes any design work.

## 02 — Business Objectives

- **Three concrete channels to find the objectives, pick by your role:** (1) read it straight off the design brief; (2) if you're at an agency, ask your producer/creative director directly what outcomes the project is chasing; (3) if you're freelance or senior, ask the client directly in a meeting.
- **The "why" laddering demo (verbatim shape):**
  Client: "I want a responsive website." → You: "Why?" → Client: "So it works well on mobile." → You: "Why?" → Client: "So we get more traffic." → You: "Why?" → Client: "So more people buy from our store and we increase sales." — **that's** the real objective. Expect roughly **three rounds of "why"** to get from a feature request down to a business outcome. Stop laddering once the answer is stated in outcome terms (revenue, conversion, retention), not feature terms (responsive, fast, modern).
  - Rule of thumb: it feels confrontational the first few times you do it; clients respect it once they see where it lands.
- **Self-practice drill (do this before every new project, not just once):** take an old project, extract no more than 5 macro goals, then literally record yourself on audio or video pitching the design decisions back in business-objective language as if presenting to the client. Listen back. Fix anywhere you sound unclear or hand-wavy. Repeat. This is a rehearsal technique for the "tap on the shoulder" test, not a one-off exercise.

## 03 — User Insight

- **Tool-credibility note:** the instructor names Inspectlet specifically and states that every time he's demoed session-replay/flow data to a client, "their mind has been blown" — session-recording tools are called out as a reliably strong trust-builder early in a relationship, worth demonstrating rather than just describing.
- **Explicit ethics line on observation:** don't be a peeping Tom, don't be too in-the-user's-face either — "don't be a creep" is the literal bar.
- **Verbatim-language technique for interview/survey synthesis:** when summarizing qualitative responses, quote respondents' own words rather than paraphrasing them. Worked example: in the course's own student survey, responses like "earn more money" and "earn a boatload of cash" were kept in the respondents' own phrasing and used directly to both validate and *adjust* the course curriculum — the synthesis step isn't just categorizing, it's preserving the user's actual language so intent doesn't get flattened.
- **Journey map worked example (the wedding tie story):** instructor gets home from work, realizes he needs a tie for a friend's wedding that weekend and needs it shipped in time. He walks through: feeling of urgency → sits on couch, opens laptop → browses several competitor sites first → lands on the client's site → narrates his frame of mind at each step (relaxed vs. urgent, confused by pricing, etc.). **Rule: map everything that happens *before* the user reaches your product, not just the on-site flow — and annotate emotional state at each step, not only the click path.**
- **"White coat syndrome" (named term):** the risk in formal usability testing — sitting someone down for a "test" makes them feel like they have to perform to give the "right" answer, producing unnatural behavior. Prefer gathering data in the user's real environment when you can get access to it.
- **Persona-scale opinion:** big agencies with big budgets build out "wacky," over-detailed personas — instructor's stance is a single simple card is enough. Also states explicitly: **not every project needs a persona at all** — sometimes go straight from raw data synthesis to design without building one.

## 04 — Layers of Design

- **Concrete structure-layer examples:** e-commerce flow = homepage → product listing → product detail → checkout. App flow = Tinder-style swipe interface, where the swipe gesture itself *is* the interaction layer.
- **Explicit, opinionated rejection of the sequential UX-then-UI agency model:** instructor states plainly he does not believe UX and UI should be split into a relay — wireframe, user-test, then "color it in." Reasoning given: visual decisions (color, imagery, video richness) change how hierarchy actually reads and feels; low-fidelity wireframes can't reveal true hierarchy because that richness is missing. **Practical rule: work structure, interaction, and visual concurrently on the same problem — don't hand off a "UX-approved" skeleton for someone else to skin.**

## 05 — Design Process (full worked example, richest video — Vespy e-commerce brief)

This lesson is a single end-to-end walkthrough of a fictional-but-composite real brief. The value is in the *techniques* demonstrated, not the fictional brand's specifics.

**Brief-triage techniques:**
- Physically highlight/circle the load-bearing phrases in a client's brief answer and ignore the fluff around them (worked example: from a long paragraph, pulled out "simplicity," "quality," "2010," "started in a bedroom," "two stores," "American made," "medium-to-high tier," "basic silhouettes" as the actual signal).
- When a client explains their own company poorly, don't only send a follow-up email — go do independent research in parallel (their site, catalogs, collateral, social channels).
- Hand-write a brain-dump in a notebook while reading the brief, even messily. The act of writing is what processes the information — it's not just a record-keeping step.
- When a client says "unsure of exact numbers" for target metrics, don't stall waiting for them — go find proxy numbers yourself. Worked example: checked the client's social following directly (**50K followers total across channels, 35K of that on Instagram**) to gauge real scale before recommending a social-driven traffic strategy.
- Audit the client's own social *followers*, not just their content — click into individual follower profiles to see who is actually buying into the brand's lifestyle (worked example: minimalist "Kinfolk reader" aesthetic in followers' own feeds signaled the real demographic before any formal research).
- When a client gives reference sites they like, extract the *essence* of why (worked example: client liked a reference site for "simplicity and product photography" specifically — those two words, not the overall look, are what to carry forward).
- If a client skips a question outright (worked example: asked for competitor URLs, got none back), follow up explicitly rather than proceeding without the data.
- Call out generic demographic answers as insufficient rather than accepting them. Worked example: client's "typical user" answer was "female 25–35, young professional, income $50–120K, follows social trends" — instructor's verdict: this is generic enough to slap onto any brand and isn't useful on its own; dig deeper with real user-insight tools instead of designing off it.

**Brand/lifestyle framing (a genuine "why" lesson):** people buy into the brand and the lifestyle it represents, not just the SKU — Nike sells Michael Jordan and Roger Federer, not shoes; Rolex sells a James Bond fantasy, not a watch. Practical implication: weight how much lifestyle imagery/storytelling content gets relative to plain product shots based on how strongly the brand trades on identity vs. utility.

**Stakeholder-scale note:** typical small/medium project has 1–2 real stakeholders (e.g. a day-to-day marketing contact plus a founder for final sign-off); larger orgs can run 4–7 (department heads plus one final decision-maker).

**Research-effort timebox rule (a genuinely new numeric rule of thumb):** scale research depth to the project's total length — a **2-week sprint cannot spend 4 days on research**; a **6-month project might reasonably spend 2 weeks** on it. On short sprints, compress: hypothesize from quick channel checks (social, analytics, one store visit) and defer validation to testing later, rather than treating user research as a fixed-size ritual every project owes.

**Offline-inspiration rule (directly actionable against generic/templated design):** deliberately go to print magazines and books *before* going to digital references — named examples: Mindfood, Monocle, men's style magazines. Reasoning stated explicitly: build your own curated, private inspiration library instead of only pulling from Dribbble/Behance, specifically so your solutions don't look like everyone else's "popular reference" output. Only after mining offline sources does he move to online functional-pattern references (GitHub, CodePen) for interaction patterns.

**Contextual-research recruiting tactic:** to get realistic in-home research access, offer an incentive (worked example: **$1,000 of store credit** as a competition prize) in exchange for permission to shadow a real or prospective customer for about an hour in their actual shopping environment, having them think out loud. A concrete recruiting method for contextual inquiry beyond scheduled usability-test sessions.

**Persona worked example — 4 archetypes named by *behavior*, not demographics** (the model to copy: name the functional role, then hang the demographic detail off it, not the reverse):
- **The Hunter** (Dan, 32) — mission-driven, hunting one specific item/deal (e.g. "a formal but casual watch, $500–1000," "jeans, 32" waist, long length"); shops mainly on desktop/iMac, mobile only when desperate; sneakerhead/festival-goer profile.
- **The Explorer** (Ashley T) — browses purely for inspiration; mobile-first, phone is "the first thing she sees in the morning and the last thing at night"; window-shops multiple brands rather than converting immediately.
- **The Fan** (Amber, 21) — loyal across every channel (Snapchat/YouTube/Instagram) but low disposable income; saves specifically to buy 1–2 pieces per season; shops via laptop.
- **The Visitor/Guest** (Jenny 25, Jeremy 29) — discovers the brand incidentally via Instagram, has never heard of it before, checks the site on mobile out of curiosity, doesn't yet know if it fits their aesthetic.

**User-story format worked at higher granularity than the written distillation's single example:** "As a hunter, Dan is looking for jeans that are 32-inch waist and long length." / "As a hunter, Dan is looking for shipping times." / "As a fan, Amber is looking for discounts." / "As a guest, Jenny wants to see what clothes Vespy stocks." — the pattern: **[persona role] + [specific, filterable need]**, granular enough that it maps directly to a UI affordance (a filter, a shipping-time callout, a discount banner), not just a vague wish.

**Emotional-outcome mapping per funnel stage (a concrete, copyable framework):** name the target *feeling* for each stage explicitly, not just the task —
- Satisfied → after completing a purchase
- Happy → while browsing/in the store experience
- Informed → when looking for specific information
- Inspired → when consuming brand story/lifestyle content
Design decisions get checked against the feeling assigned to that stage, not only against task completion.

**Ideation mechanism worked example:** research finding ("customers love talking to real staff in-store, it's personal and community-based") converted directly into a feature concept (a live chat/store-helper feature) — this is the literal move from insight to concept, shown as a single step rather than described abstractly.

**Explicit anti-"mobile-first" stance:** instructor designs desktop and mobile *concurrently*, starting from the homepage, and says he does not default to mobile-first — he prefers to "step into the mind of the user" per context (some users are on a couch on mobile, some are at a desk) rather than apply a blanket mobile-first rule.

**Prototyping-in-flight tactic:** cut a small piece out of an in-progress concept — e.g. just the mobile menu interaction — and prototype/test that fragment early, rather than waiting for the full page or site to be finished before testing anything.

**Dev-parallelization tactic:** hand finished pages to developers to start building while later pages are still being designed, specifically to feel what the real, live product will be like early — rather than discovering "it doesn't feel right built" only at final handoff.

**Page sequencing worked example:** design order followed the priority order surfaced by user stories (home → product listing → product detail → …), but with explicit permission to loop back and revise earlier pages for consistency once later pages reveal new decisions — sequencing is directional, not one-pass.
