# Nguyen Le — Process Masterclass, Module 1: Design Thinking

Distilled from the module's PDFs (lesson notes, slide decks, cheat sheets, worksheets). Freelance/career content excluded. Focus: how to make design decisions defensible, user-grounded, and outcome-driven — the reasoning layer that should precede any visual/UI work.

## 1. Design Thinking — Overview (core frame)

**Definition:** "Design thinking is a human-centered approach to innovation that integrates the needs of people, business objectives applied through design."

**The 3 pillars — every design decision must sit at the intersection of:**
- **USERS** — their motivation, goals, objectives, pain points.
- **BUSINESS** — success metrics, key objectives, stakeholders.
- **DESIGN** — the craft that services the other two.

Named principle: *"What we do as designers is the servicing of user needs and delivering on business objectives."* Design is never decoration first; it is the delivery mechanism for the other two circles.

**Why a framework matters (capture these as working rules):**
- Use a framework to move design from **subjective to objective** — that is what makes work defensible and valuable.
- Nobody senior cares that a button is red if the design **delivered happy customers and an increase in sales**. Argue at that level, not at the pixel level.
- Treat the process as a **roadmap to discover answers, not a forced linear path** — loop back freely.
- Make cool things, but make them **solve problems people have**.
- "People don't pay you just for your final output. They pay you for your process and the outcomes it creates."

**The 5-step process (Understand → Create → Deliver arc):**
1. **Discover** — prepare research; understand who we are serving; understand the challenge; gather inspiration. *Output: a load of raw data.*
2. **Interpret** — create stories and scenarios; create personas; define clear outcomes. *Output: data turned into information.*
3. **Ideate** — generate different ideas; refine ideas. *Output: a load of ideas.*
4. **Prototype** — create quick mocks to test interactions; test ideas to create context for how things feel, look and touch. *Output: best ideas turned into working prototypes people can use.*
5. **Test** — get feedback on prototypes; continue to refine. *Output: validated prototypes — what's working, what isn't. Rinse & repeat.*

**Key takeaway:** Focus on delivery of user and business objectives. Understand the people you are servicing, prototype things and test quickly.

## 2. Business Objectives

**The tap-on-the-shoulder test:** If someone tapped you on the shoulder and asked "what were you trying to achieve with this design?" — you must be able to answer instantly, in business terms. Apply this test to every page, section, and component you ship.

**Rules:**
- Extract the **macro-level goals** of a project before designing. Keep the list to **no more than 5**.
- Tie every design decision to one of those goals. Revisions are fine **if they are for the right reasons**; superficial change requests must justify themselves against the business objectives.
- Never accept "we just want a good-looking, responsive website" as the brief. There are always underlying reasons — dig for them; it forces the big picture and makes later critique accountable ("nitpicks must be validated against objectives").
- Move conversations away from "how it looks" toward "what the project is trying to achieve."

**Cheat sheet — common website goals by department (use as a question template when extracting objectives):**
- **Marketing** — drive traffic, engage prospects, generate leads, re-engage existing customers.
- **Sales** — increase customer conversions, increase sales.
- **Customer Support** — improve customer satisfaction through better service (e.g. reduce support calls).
- **Webmasters** — simplify and optimize content updates and site management.
- **Operations** — reduce costs by streamlining and automating workflows and tasks.

Phrase discovery questions from these templates, e.g.: "Are you building responsive because mobile traffic is rising, and do you want to convert more of that traffic into paying customers?" / "Are you trying to reduce the number of calls to customer support?" / "Is a main goal to generate leads through email signups?"

**Make goals SMART** (Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Timely). Examples of correctly-formed goals:
- Increase the website's conversion rate by 5% in the next 3 months.
- Reduce average task-completion time by 20% (measured through usability testing or analytics).
- Increase traffic from social media by 15%.

## 3. User Insight

**Core premise:** You can only solve the problem when you understand the problem. "We need to have **context** to deliver great work. Understand people, their needs and what their motivations are."

**The gift analogy (why context beats budget):** Timmy loves tennis and dreams of seeing a live match. Front-row NBA tickets (expensive, impressive) get a polite "thanks." A US Open ground pass (cheaper, targeted) gets "OMG!!!" — **the right thing beats the impressive thing; spend effort where the user's actual motivation is.**

**The three escalating questions — answer in order before designing:**
1. Who are our users?
2. Why are they using our product?
3. Where, when and how are they using our product?

Empathy defined: being able to imagine yourself in the shoes of the people you are designing for.

**Discovery questions to answer with data (checklist):**
- Who are our users?
- What is currently working? What pain points are users facing?
- What frame of mind are users in when interacting with our product or service?
- What range of emotions do users display during this process?
- Where are users using the product? Where does it live, and what is the context?
- How do users interact with our product or service?

**4 ways to gather the data:**
1. **Observation & research** — watch how people interact with products, brands, experiences. For websites use session-recording tools (e.g. Inspectlet-type): observe **exact user flows, design pitfalls, what's working**. In physical retail: who visits, what they buy, what their emotion is.
2. **User interviews & surveys** — uncover underlying reasons, opinions, motivations. In interviews: uncover the pain points in the experience and what users hope to achieve.
3. **Data & analytics** — site/app data: traffic, conversion rate, bounce rates, popular pages, where users come from and where they click to.
4. **Journey maps** — the real moments of truth and the ways customers achieve their needs; understand the experience across the **full cycle of interactions**, not one screen.

**4 observation stances (choose deliberately):**
1. **Complete observer** — researcher unknown to users (pure watching, e.g. analytics/session replay).
2. **Observer as participant** — researcher known; plays a neutral role guiding specific tasks (classic usability test).
3. **Participant as observer** — researcher known; integrated into activities as a friend.
4. **Complete participant** — fully embedded, "undercover boss"; performs the same activities users perform.

**What you are looking for:** data you can interpret into a **viewpoint** that creates better outcomes for both user and business goals — not data for its own sake.

**Interpretation toolkit (formats):**
- **Persona** — name, age, job, education, social hubs, short story. Keep it one card, not a dossier.
- **Epic** — a short narrative of a real situation and motivation (Ashley invited to Coachella; wants to stand out; needs festival clothes).
- **User story** — one sentence: *[User] wants to [action with constraint], so that [outcome]* — e.g. "Ashley wants to shop for playsuits under $100 so she can get them in time for Coachella."
- **Story mapping table** — columns: Persona | Context | Mode | User goal. (Ashley T., partygoer / at home watching TV, browsing stores on phone, checking Instagram / exploring, wants a playsuit / browse, filter by price, compare — affordable and ships in time.)
- Rank user stories by **priority** (high / moderate importance) on a board: Persona → Context → Mode → User stories → Priority ranking.
- **Map micro goals (user stories) to macro goals (business objectives)** — e.g. macro "increase revenue 10% in next 6 months" ← micro "Ashley can find playsuits under $100." Every user story should ladder up to a business objective.

**Anti-bureaucracy rule:** Understanding the user is important, **but creating 100-page documents is boring and does not result in better work or outcomes.** Be aware of the process, but it's really about **listening, creating and testing things quickly with real people**. Sometimes trust your instincts and make things.

**Key takeaway:** If tapped on the shoulder and asked "what are users trying to achieve with this design?" — you must be able to answer.

## 4. Layers of Design (order of decisions)

Four layers, worked in this order (ref: Intercom). Use this as the sequence for any UI/feature work:

1. **Outcome** — what outcomes do we hope to achieve for the business and the users? (Ties directly to Business Objectives + User Insight.)
2. **Structure** — design a system: work out the required components to meet the intended outcome, and map the relationships between them.
3. **Interaction** — the microinteractions, the sequences of behaviour and events; the UI components and how people interact with and manipulate them.
4. **Visual** — only now blend the thinking into execution with **colour, grids, typography etc.**

**Rules:**
- Never start at layer 4. Visual polish on top of a wrong structure or unclear outcome is wasted work.
- Structure, interaction and visual **influence each other and can happen concurrently** — keep iterating and **have the courage to change your mind**.
- "It's a messy process, but amongst the chaos there's a method to the madness" — the straight-line diagram is a lie; the scribble is real. Plan for loops.

## 5. Design Thinking Workflow (step-guide worksheet)

A concrete per-project checklist, phase by phase:

**1. Discovery**
1. Extract the brief & run a kickoff.
2. Identify the top-level business objectives.
3. Identify who we're servicing; list existing user insight/data; outline the user groups.
4. Collect user insight via: Observation, Interviews & Surveys, Analytics & Data, Journey Mapping.
5. List other projects that already solved these problems — **both functional and aesthetic reference points**.
6. Record other variables: timelines, milestones, stakeholders, competitors, technological requirements, assets, branding style guide. Record any data that enables informed design decisions.

**2. Interpretation**
1. Interpret user-insight data; create personas if applicable.
2. List the **top actions users are trying to perform** (most important first); create user stories and epics.
3. Define outcomes to achieve — and how the user should **feel** while performing and completing the action.

**3. Ideation**
1. Translate interpretation into design solutions. Go wide: look at **conventional design patterns first**, then groundbreaking ideas. Sticky notes, sketches, comps — whatever medium conveys the idea fastest.
2. Select best ideas (collectively or via key decision-makers), or test a wide breadth with prototypes. **Design → prototype → test in small iterative loops.**
3. Concurrently create structure and a system: IA, sitemap, prioritise key user stories and flows logically. Test assumptions with prototypes.
4. Start the visual design system: moodboards, styleboards, concept designs.

**4. Prototype**
- Context is everything: prototypes communicate what static mocks can't — how something feels, looks, behaves in the environment where it will actually live. Low-fi (paper, barebones structure) to high-fi (clickable tools).

**5. Test**
- Put prototypes in front of real people — ideally actual end users. Record what's working and what isn't; go back and ideate from the results.

## 6. Design Sprint Methods (Google playbook)

Framework for solving and testing a design problem in **2–5 days**. Useful even solo/small-team as a decision-forcing structure.

**Planning rules:**
- **Plan 1 day of prep for every day of sprint time.**
- Write a **design challenge statement** first; all work and testing hangs off it. A good statement is: (1) **relevant** — tied to team goals, (2) **concise**, (3) **inspiring**, and focused on a **target audience/segment**, with explicit **deliverables**. Example: "Design an intuitive tablet reading experience app for children age 4–7, focused on Q4 launch. Deliverables: polished mocks and a clickable prototype for testing."
- Ideal team size **5–8** (designers, engineers, PM, experts, researcher, prototyper). Split larger groups.
- Before starting on an existing product, run a **design audit**: interview key stakeholders; review all existing documents; review all relevant user research; review current designs; identify core use cases.

**The 6 sprint stages (superset of the 5-step process):**
1. **Understand** — user needs, business needs, technology capacities.
2. **Define** — the key strategy and focus.
3. **Diverge** — explore as many ideas as possible.
4. **Decide** — select the best ideas so far.
5. **Prototype** — build an artifact that lets you test with users.
6. **Validate** — test with users, business stakeholders, technical experts.

There are 40+ methods; **never use all of them** — select per sprint.

**Understand methods:** 360° lightning talks (business goals & success metrics / 5 min; technical capacities & challenges / 5 min; relevant user research / 5 min); user interviews (how they use the product, likes/dislikes; for new products: what alternatives they use today); field visits (interview + real context); **stakeholder map** (list all stakeholders 10 min → group 2 min → decide who you design for and in what order); **competitive overview** — brief review of **3–10 similar projects**, listing likes and dislikes; summarize learnings with sticky notes, group into themes, vote ("first check," not final).

**Define methods:**
- **Central user journey** — map stages from Discovery → First-time use → Return use → Expert use.
- **Design principles** — "What 3 words should users describe the product with?" List candidates individually, select as a team. After testing, ask users for their 3 words and **compare to the intention** (a measurable taste check).
- **First tweet** — write the launch announcement in ≤140 characters to force strategy into one sentence.

**Diverge methods (timed):** 8 ideas in 5 min (fold paper into 8 rectangles, one sketch each — warm-up); 1 big idea in 5 min; 1 storyboard in 5 min (key user steps, comic-strip style) for flows too complex for one frame.

**Decide methods:** **Zen voting** — review and vote in silence first so no one is biased by others; then team review & decision on what to prototype; **Thinking hats** for biased/new teams — assign perspectives (Idea Generator, Optimist, Pessimist, Technical feasibility, User Advocate).

**Prototype:** something "real enough to feel" — mock, demo, video, or physical prototype. Teams spend the most time here.

**Validate (three checks, not one):**
1. **User test** — what do users like/dislike, what would they improve, does it meet their needs?
2. **Stakeholder validation** — the person who funds/allocates resources must review and approve.
3. **Technical feasibility check** — engineering review: do the ideas match team capacity? Scope and discuss workarounds.

After the sprint: create a path to launch, document, send a summary, survey participants, plan the next one.

## Top rules from this unit

1. Every design decision must serve the intersection of user needs, business objectives, and design craft — if it serves only aesthetics, cut or rethink it.
2. Pass the tap-on-the-shoulder test at all times: for any page or component, be able to state instantly what it is trying to achieve for the business and for the user.
3. Extract the macro business goals before designing — a maximum of 5 — and trace every design choice back to one of them.
4. Refuse "we just want a good-looking website" as a brief; convert it into department-level goals (marketing: leads/traffic; sales: conversions; support: fewer calls; operations: lower cost).
5. Write goals as SMART targets with numbers and deadlines, e.g. "increase conversion rate by 5% in 3 months," "cut task-completion time 20%."
6. Answer three questions, in order, before designing: Who are the users? Why are they using this? Where, when and how are they using it?
7. Give people the right thing, not the impressive thing — a cheap, well-targeted solution beats an expensive misread of what users actually want (US Open ground pass > front-row NBA tickets for a tennis fan).
8. Gather insight through four channels: observation (session recordings → exact flows, pitfalls), interviews/surveys (motivations, pain points), analytics (traffic, conversion, bounce, click paths), and journey maps (full cycle of interactions).
9. Turn data into a viewpoint: one-card personas, short epics, and one-sentence user stories ("X wants to [action + constraint] so that [outcome]"), ranked by priority.
10. Map every micro goal (user story) to a macro goal (business objective); delete work that ladders up to nothing.
11. Don't produce 100-page research documents — they don't improve outcomes. Listen, create, and test quickly with real people instead.
12. Work the four layers in order: Outcome → Structure → Interaction → Visual. Colour, grids, and typography come last, after the outcome and system are settled.
13. Structure, interaction, and visual influence each other — iterate concurrently and have the courage to change your mind; the process is loops, not a line.
14. In ideation, go wide but start from conventional design patterns before reaching for novel ones.
15. Design → prototype → test in small iterative loops; a prototype is anything "real enough to feel," and context (the real environment/device) is everything.
16. Anchor projects to a challenge statement that is relevant, concise, inspiring, names the target audience, and specifies deliverables.
17. Start any work on an existing product with a design audit: stakeholder interviews, existing docs, prior user research, current designs, core use cases.
18. Run a competitive overview of 3–10 comparable products, explicitly listing likes and dislikes, before generating ideas.
19. Define 3 words users should use to describe the product; after testing, ask users for their 3 words and compare — a measurable check on design intent.
20. Validation is three-sided: users (needs met?), the stakeholder who funds it (approved?), and engineering (feasible within capacity?). All three, every time; vote on ideas in silence first to avoid bias.
